# japanese-writing

The goal: a reader with none of the writer's context reads the text once, from top to bottom, and understands and can use exactly what the source means.

- The source
    - When revising, the original text
    - When writing, the material and the request
- Purpose, reader, and medium
    - Infer them when unstated.
    - Ask only when a wrong guess would change what the text must say.

Judge every choice by that reader.

- The meaning is exact
    - Every fact, claim, stance, condition, limit, exception, negation, cause, degree of certainty, actor, object, and source the source holds is kept.
    - A summary drops only what the purpose does not need.
    - Nothing the source does not hold is added: no fact, example, reason, opinion, or voice.
    - Plainer wording, or a word the context allows only one reading of, is not an addition. Neither is a sentence that only introduces or links what the source holds.
    - What the source says is unknown stays unknown, and the text says so where the reader needs it. Where the source says nothing, the text adds neither an answer nor a doubt, and items the source only places in sequence are linked by time, not by cause.
    - Emphasis or praise that states no fact is not meaning, and goes.
- The shape fits how the reader uses the text
    - Text the reader looks things up in, such as a README, a notice, a spec, or a summary, takes the shape of its content. Parallel items become a list, items compared on the same attributes a table, ordered steps a numbered list, and separate topics sections under headings.
    - Text the reader follows from start to end, such as an argument, an essay, a paper, an article, a letter, or a story, stays prose, because each sentence builds on the one before. A paragraph carries one step of the thought through to its point. Notes given as material are not a paragraph plan: one paragraph usually spans several of their items, such as an action and what followed it. A paragraph of one or two short sentences reads as a list item turned into prose. A paragraph's first sentence says what it is about, and each paragraph follows from the one before.
    - Hierarchy is shown by headings, order, and indentation. What belongs under a label or an item is indented beneath it. Where the medium has no headings, indentation alone carries the hierarchy.
    - Bold, decorative brackets, and symbols never mark sections or importance.
    - Writing or revising may change the shape and the order, but no placement implies a relation the source does not state.
- Each line is taken in at a glance
    - A sentence or list item states one thing. One that carries two unrelated things becomes two.
    - Cause, condition, purpose, exception, and contrast are stated, not left for the reader to infer. This holds between sentences too: a run of short sentences that leaves the reader to guess how they connect fails as a long sentence does.
    - Nothing makes the reader jump into an aside and back. What a bracket or dash would hold becomes its own sentence or item; it goes only from a summary whose purpose does not need it.
- Every phrase parses one way
    - Words fit the context and collocate naturally.
    - Each phrase sits close to what it modifies.
    - Commas are as few as parsing in one pass allows. One goes where a clause or long modifier ends and the next begins, or where a phrase would otherwise attach to the wrong word. None follows a short topic, subject, or phrase, or a sentence-opening connective, unless the reader would misparse without it.
    - Japanese omits subjects and objects. State one only when the reader could not otherwise tell who does what.
- The medium's conventions hold
    - No ASCII space beside Latin letters or digits inside Japanese unless the medium requires one.

Brevity, rhythm, and style come after these. Keep deliberate rhetoric where the purpose calls for it.
